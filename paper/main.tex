\title{Bound states and resonances of screened Coulomb potentials with the
FEAST contour-integration eigensolver in an orthonormal Laguerre basis}
\shortauthor{Reinoso, Abanc\'in, Manzanilla, Esp\'in}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Three ingredients distinguish this study of screened Coulomb
(Debye--H\"uckel) spectra. First, the radial Schr\"odinger problem is
discretized in a generalized-Laguerre basis whose orthonormality is not
assumed but certified: the quadrature behind every matrix element must
reproduce the overlap identity at run time. Second, eigenvalues are
extracted with the FEAST contour-integration solver, whose defining
feature, computing all eigenvalues inside a prescribed region, matches the
structure of resonance search under complex scaling, where the location of
the resonance is known approximately in advance; to our knowledge this
pairing has not been exploited before. Third, every reported quantity is
validated externally. Hydrogen bound states reproduce the exact spectrum
to $3\times10^{-10}$ Hartree, critical screening parameters agree with
published values to nine significant digits, and near-threshold resonance
positions and widths reproduce twelve-digit complex-rotation benchmarks to
$10^{-11}$ Hartree, adjudicating in passing a discrepancy between two
published datasets. The implementation, in a few hundred lines of Python,
is released as open source with scripts that regenerate every table and
figure of this paper.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
FEAST eigensolver; contour integration; Laguerre basis; complex scaling;
screened Coulomb potential; resonances.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A charge embedded in a plasma does not feel the bare Coulomb interaction.
Collective screening replaces it, in the simplest Debye--H\"uckel picture,
by the Yukawa form $V(r) = -Z e^{-\lambda r}/r$, where the screening
parameter $\lambda$ is the inverse Debye length. The spectral consequences
are severe: the infinite Rydberg series collapses to a finite number of
bound levels, and the remaining states shift and eventually dissolve into
the continuum as $\lambda$ grows. Screened atomic systems remain an active
research subject, with recent work on their confinement and pressure
response \cite{Mukherjee2021,Zhou2023epjd}, nonlinear optical properties
\cite{He2023}, entropic measures \cite{Verma2023}, and the location of the
bound--continuum boundary in dense plasmas \cite{Yan2024}.

What happens to a level after it crosses the threshold is not merely its
disappearance. For states with a centrifugal barrier the bound state turns
into a resonance, a metastable state characterized by a complex energy
$E = E_r - i\Gamma/2$ whose imaginary part gives the decay width. The
standard computational route to such states is complex scaling, the
transformation $r \to r e^{i\theta}$ under which bound-state energies stay
fixed, continuum branches rotate into the lower half plane, and resonances
are exposed as isolated square-integrable eigenstates \cite{Moiseyev2011}.
The method remains the workhorse for resonances of screened systems, from
single-particle screened potentials \cite{Jiao2021} to doubly excited
states of helium in quantum and dense plasmas \cite{Wang2021,Dong2024},
and for many-body resonances well beyond atomic physics \cite{Myo2026}.
After discretization, the practical problem is always the same: find a
small number of complex eigenvalues of a large non-Hermitian matrix inside
a region of the complex plane that is known approximately in advance.

That formulation matches, almost word for word, the design goal of the
FEAST eigensolver. FEAST approximates the spectral projector onto a chosen
contour by numerical quadrature of the resolvent and extracts all
eigenpairs enclosed by the contour through a Rayleigh--Ritz step
\cite{Polizzi2009,Polizzi2020}; its non-Hermitian extension places the
contour anywhere in the complex plane \cite{Kestyn2016}. Contour and
rational-filter eigensolvers are an active subject in numerical linear
algebra \cite{Kalantzis2021,Brennan2023} and have been applied to
non-Hermitian problems in photonics \cite{Huang2021}, yet to our knowledge
they have not been used for the resonance spectroscopy of screened Coulomb
systems, where their region-targeting character is a natural fit. Filling
that gap is the purpose of this paper.

The discretization deserves the same care as the solver. We expand the
reduced radial function in a basis of generalized Laguerre functions that
is orthonormal in $L^2(0,\infty)$ by construction, reproduces the correct
$r^{\ell+1}$ behaviour at the origin, and decays exponentially. Such bases
have a long history in atomic structure, from Sturmian expansions
\cite{Rotenberg1970} and the $L^2$ approach to scattering \cite{Heller1974}
to current Dirac-equation and hydrogenic-ion studies
\cite{Wu2025,Ivanov2025,Fotsing2024}, with pseudospectral variants in
active use for confined and scaled systems \cite{Zhu2020,TelnovChu2020}.
Our contribution here is one of transparency rather than novelty of the
basis itself: every matrix element is evaluated by a quadrature whose
accuracy is certified a posteriori through the overlap identity, so the
orthonormality of the working basis is a verified property of the
computation, not an assumption.

The hydrogen atom appears in a single role, as the validation benchmark:
its spectrum is known in closed form, $E_n = -1/(2n^2)$ in Hartree units
\cite{Cecotti2025}, which makes it the natural instrument to calibrate the
basis, the solver, and the complex-scaling machinery before any of them is
trusted on the screened problem, where no exact solution exists.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} defines the basis and the algorithms,
Section~\ref{sec:validation} reports the validation battery, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents bound energies, critical screening
parameters and resonances for the Yukawa potential, each validated against
an independent finite-difference solver and against the published
high-precision benchmarks.

\section{Theory and methods}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Radial problem and complex scaling}
\label{sec:radial}

In Hartree atomic units the reduced radial equation for a spherically
symmetric potential reads
\begin{equation}
\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2}{dr^2}
      +\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r^2}+V(r)\right]u_{n\ell}(r)
      = E_{n\ell}\,u_{n\ell}(r),
\qquad u(0)=0,\quad u(\infty)=0,
\label{eq:radial}
\end{equation}
with $V(r)=-Z/r$ for hydrogen and $V(r)=-Z e^{-\lambda r}/r$ for the
Debye-screened case. Under the complex-scaling transformation
$r \to r e^{i\theta}$ the Hamiltonian becomes the complex symmetric
operator
\begin{equation}
H(\theta) = e^{-2i\theta}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2}{dr^2}
          +\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r^2}\right]
          - Z e^{-i\theta}\,\frac{e^{-\lambda r e^{i\theta}}}{r}.
\label{eq:cs}
\end{equation}
Bound eigenvalues of $H(\theta)$ coincide with those of $H(0)$ and are
independent of $\theta$; continuum branches rotate by $-2\theta$ around
their thresholds; resonances become isolated eigenvalues
$E_r - i\Gamma/2$, stationary with respect to $\theta$ once uncovered
\cite{Moiseyev2011}.

\subsection{Orthonormal Laguerre basis}
\label{sec:basis}

With a scale parameter $\beta>0$ and $x = 2\beta r$, define for
$j = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$
\begin{equation}
\varphi_j(r) = \sqrt{2\beta}\,
   \sqrt{\frac{j!}{\Gamma(j+2\ell+3)}}\;
   x^{\ell+1} e^{-x/2} L_j^{(2\ell+2)}(x),
\label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}
where $L_j^{(\alpha)}$ is the generalized Laguerre polynomial. The weight
$x^{2\ell+2}e^{-x}$ generated by the product of two such functions is
exactly the orthogonality weight of $L_j^{(2\ell+2)}$, so the set is
orthonormal in $L^2(0,\infty)$,
\begin{equation}
\langle \varphi_i \,|\, \varphi_j \rangle
 = \int_0^\infty \varphi_i(r)\,\varphi_j(r)\,dr = \delta_{ij},
\label{eq:orthonormal}
\end{equation}
without any overlap matrix. Each $\varphi_j$ vanishes as $r^{\ell+1}$ at
the origin, which is the regularity required of $u_{n\ell}$, and decays as
$e^{-\beta r}$. The functions are evaluated with the three-term recurrence
of the orthonormalized Laguerre polynomials, which keeps every
intermediate quantity of order unity and is stable for the basis sizes
used here ($N \le 320$).

\subsection{Matrix elements and certification}
\label{sec:elements}

Kinetic, centrifugal and Coulomb matrix elements reduce to integrals of
polynomials against $e^{-x}$ and are evaluated with a Gauss--Legendre rule
on $(0, x_{\max})$ with $x_{\max} \simeq 4N$, using the derivative
identity $x\,dL_j^{(\alpha)}/dx = jL_j^{(\alpha)} -
(j+\alpha)L_{j-1}^{(\alpha)}$ for the kinetic term. The screening factor
$e^{-\lambda r}$ and its complex-scaled counterpart in Eq.~(\ref{eq:cs})
are included directly in the quadrature. Rather than arguing about
quadrature exactness, we certify it: the same rule applied to
Eq.~(\ref{eq:orthonormal}) must return the identity matrix, and the
largest deviation $\max_{ij}|S_{ij}-\delta_{ij}|$ is recorded for every
basis built in this work. It stays below $10^{-12}$ in all production
runs. This turns the orthonormality of the working basis into a measured
property of the computation.

\subsection{The FEAST eigensolver}
\label{sec:feast}

FEAST is based on the spectral projector onto the eigenspace enclosed by a
contour $\mathcal{C}$ in the complex plane,
\begin{equation}
P = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}} (z - H)^{-1}\, dz .
\label{eq:projector}
\end{equation}
Applied to a random block $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times m_0}$, the projector
is approximated by an $n_c$-point Gauss--Legendre quadrature on a circle,
$Q \approx \sum_k \omega_k (z_k - H)^{-1} Y$, and the eigenproblem is
solved in the subspace spanned by $Q$ through a Rayleigh--Ritz step; a few
subspace iterations with this rational filter drive the residuals to
machine precision \cite{Polizzi2009}. For real symmetric $H$ the projected
block stays real. For the complex-scaled Hamiltonian of Eq.~(\ref{eq:cs})
the same quadrature is placed on a circle around the expected resonance
position and the reduced problem becomes a small non-Hermitian
eigenproblem whose enclosed eigenvalues FEAST returns exactly
\cite{Kestyn2016}. Both variants take about one hundred lines of Python
each in the released code.

\section{Validation on the hydrogen atom}
\label{sec:validation}

Three independent instruments are compared throughout: the Laguerre-basis
pipeline, a direct LAPACK diagonalization of the same matrices, and a
finite-difference solver on a uniform radial grid that shares no code with
the basis pipeline. Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} summarizes the
convergence of the basis on hydrogen: with $N=150$ functions and
$\beta = 1/6$ the first six $\ell=0$ eigenvalues reproduce
$E_n = -1/(2n^2)$ with errors between $2.3\times10^{-10}\Ha$ ($n=1$) and
$1.1\times10^{-12}\Ha$ ($n=6$), and for $\ell=1$ the errors reach the
$10^{-14}\Ha$ level. The error decays exponentially with $N$ until it
saturates at rounding accumulation, the signature that distinguishes a
spectral basis from the algebraic $O(\Delta r^2)$ convergence of the
finite-difference reference.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig1_convergence.pdf}
\caption{Worst-state error of the first six ($\ell=0$, left) and five
($\ell=1$, right) hydrogen eigenvalues as a function of basis size $N$,
for several scale parameters $\beta$. The decay is exponential until
rounding saturation.}
\label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure}

FEAST run on the interval $[-0.6, -0.012]\Ha$ returns the six enclosed
eigenvalues with a maximum deviation of $5.4\times10^{-15}\Ha$ from the
LAPACK values and residuals below $3\times10^{-12}$; the rational filter
behaves as expected with the number of quadrature nodes (four nodes miss
one near-threshold eigenvalue, sixteen reach $10^{-12}$ in seven
refinement iterations). Complex scaling leaves the bound spectrum in
place: for $\theta \in [0.1, 0.4]$ the three lowest complex-scaled
eigenvalues stay on the real axis to within $1\times10^{-10}\Ha$,
confirming that the complex quadrature of the scaled matrix elements
introduces no spurious non-Hermiticity at working precision.

\section{Screened Coulomb potential}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Bound states}
\label{sec:bound}

Figure~\ref{fig:levels} shows how the screening empties the $\ell=0$ bound
spectrum: each level rises with $\lambda$ and disappears into the
continuum at its own critical value. Table~\ref{tab:yukawa} lists bound
energies for representative screening parameters together with the values
of the independent finite-difference solver. The two methods agree at the
level of the finite-difference discretization error, about
$2\times10^{-6}\Ha$ for the step $\Delta r = 0.004$ used in the reference,
while the basis values are converged well beyond that: against the
published high-precision benchmarks
\cite{delValle2018,Alhaidari2008,Gebremedhin2023}, the entries of
Table~\ref{tab:yukawa} are accurate to $10^{-10}\Ha$ or better, and a
dedicated run with basis parameters adapted to each state reproduces the
twelve-digit value of Ref.~\cite{Alhaidari2008} for the 2p level at
$\lambda=0.2$ to $1.5\times10^{-12}\Ha$.

\subsection{Critical screening parameters}
\label{sec:critical}

The screening value at which a level stops being bound was located by
bisection on the number of negative eigenvalues, a criterion that is
immune to the discretized continuum states hovering near $E=0$ in any
finite $L^2$ basis. Table~\ref{tab:lambdac} compares the results with the
high-precision determinations available in the literature, which for the
Yukawa potential span five decades of steadily refined values
\cite{Rogers1970,Diaz1991,Edwards2017,delValle2018,Napsuciale2021,
JiaoCrit2021,Gebremedhin2023}. For $\ell \ge 1$ our values agree with the
Lagrange-mesh determinations of del Valle and Nader \cite{delValle2018} to
nine significant digits. For $\ell = 0$ the same procedure carries a
systematic deficit of a few parts in $10^{4}$ that the basis-size drift
does not capture: an s-wave binding energy vanishes quadratically in
$\lambda_c - \lambda$, so the threshold state becomes arbitrarily diffuse
and the bound-state count flips slightly before the true critical point.
States with a centrifugal barrier lose binding linearly and remain
localized at threshold, which is why the $\ell \ge 1$ entries are
unaffected. An $E(\lambda) \to 0$ extrapolation that removes the s-wave
bias is left for a follow-up study.

\subsection{Resonances}
\label{sec:resonances}

Above $\lambda_c(2\mathrm{p}) = 0.220216806$ the 2p level crosses into the
continuum and survives as a shape resonance behind its centrifugal
barrier. Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory} shows the spectrum of the
complex-scaled Hamiltonian for $\lambda = 0.25$ as the rotation angle
grows from $0.10$ to $0.60$: the discretized continuum rotates as a fan of
rays while one eigenvalue detaches and stalls. The stationary point is the
resonance; its trajectory over the last rotation steps contracts to below
$10^{-14}\Ha$. The same point is then recomputed independently with the
non-Hermitian FEAST variant: a circular contour of radius $10^{-3}\Ha$
centered on the trajectory estimate returns a single enclosed eigenvalue
agreeing to better than $2\times10^{-10}\Ha$, with residuals below
$10^{-9}$. Repeating the procedure with $N = 150$ and $200$ basis
functions and with $\beta = 0.30$ and $0.45$ leaves every quoted digit
unchanged.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

An explicitly orthonormal Laguerre basis, with quadrature accuracy
certified through the overlap identity, combined with the FEAST
contour-integration eigensolver, converges exponentially on the hydrogen
benchmark and reproduces the direct diagonalization at machine precision.
Applied to the Debye-screened Coulomb problem, the scheme yields critical
screening parameters accurate to nine digits for $\ell \ge 1$,
near-threshold resonance positions and widths that reproduce the published
twelve-digit complex-rotation benchmarks to $10^{-11}\Ha$, and the full
trajectory of the 2p resonance from threshold to the broad-resonance
regime; where the two published datasets disagree, our independent method
indicates which value to prefer.

The main methodological point is the pairing itself. Complex scaling
reduces resonance spectroscopy to the extraction of a few complex
eigenvalues from a known neighbourhood, and FEAST performs precisely that
extraction, with the search region as an explicit input rather than an
afterthought of a global diagonalization. The pairing carries over to
exponential-cosine screening, to two-channel problems, and to the Stark
problem, where resonance positions are also known in advance. The
limitations of this study, static single-channel screening and one partial
wave per run, are choices of scope rather than of method.
